Optimizing Weak Supervision from Radiology Reports for Multi-Label Knee MRI ClassificationAutomating the multi-label interpretation of multi-sequence knee magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) across 12 clinically significant findings—anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tear, medial collateral ligament (MCL) tear, medial meniscus tear, lateral meniscus tear, medial osteoarthritis (OA), lateral OA, patellofemoral OA, joint effusion, synovitis, Baker's cyst, bone contusion, and fracture—presents distinct challenges in medical vision and clinical natural language processing. The asymmetric machine learning paradigm under consideration utilizes paired free-text radiology reports solely during the training phase to supervise an image-based classifier, while final test evaluation is conducted exclusively on volumetric pixel arrays scored by macro Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC) against expert radiologist ground truth.Maximizing downstream image discriminative performance requires optimizing the fidelity of label extraction, incorporating non-binary supervisory nuances, mitigating clinical report omissions and noise, and exploiting multimodal representation learning. Furthermore, deployment within a commercial cash-prize competition requires rigorous verification that all utilized neural architectures, software codebases, and pre-trained model weights are legally unencumbered by non-commercial or academic-only licensing restrictions.Specialized Radiology Report Parsers versus Frontier Large Language ModelsAutomated extraction of structural findings from radiology text began with symbolic, rule-based systems such as CheXpert and NegBio. These algorithms rely on fixed regular expressions, universal dependency parse trees, and hand-crafted lexicons to detect the presence, absence, or uncertain mention of specific pathological concepts. While highly interpretable, rule-based architectures display severe syntactic fragility, frequently misinterpreting ambiguous phrasing, nested clinical clauses, and atypical negations.To overcome these syntactic bottlenecks, CheXbert trained a domain-adapted transformer encoder (BioBERT) on the predictions of rule-based labelers, fine-tuning it on a small corpus of expert-annotated chest radiograph reports augmented with backtranslation. CheXbert achieved a statistically significant F1 gain of $+0.055$ ($95\%\text{ CI: } 0.039, 0.070$) over rule-based methods on external test sets, approaching the diagnostic consistency of practicing radiologists. Similarly, RadGraph framed clinical text structuring as an information extraction task, identifying discrete clinical entities (such as anatomical structures and pathological observations) and predicting directed relations (such as anatomical modification or diagnostic presence) across thoracic narratives.Despite their success in chest radiography, legacy domain-specific parsers exhibit severe limitations when applied to musculoskeletal (MSK) knee MRI narratives:The primary failure mode is severe ontological and anatomical mismatch. CheXbert is hardcoded to 14 thoracic disease categories, including cardiomegaly, consolidation, and pneumothorax. RadGraph was annotated, tuned, and evaluated on chest radiograph reports from MIMIC-CXR. Neither framework possesses class categories, specialized entity vocabulary, or token embeddings adapted to musculoskeletal pathology, such as anterior versus posterior meniscal horn morphology, collateral ligament sprains, or tricompartmental cartilage degeneration. Adapting CheXbert to knee MRI requires constructing new labeled datasets, designing task-specific multi-head linear architectures, and retraining from scratch, entirely negating the utility of its off-the-shelf checkpoints.The secondary failure mode involves complex syntactic ambiguity in multi-compartment examinations. Knee MRI reports describe intricate structural interrelationships across distinct compartments, multi-sequence signal characteristics (such as T1-weighted hypointensity and fluid-sensitive fat-suppressed hyperintensity), and comparative chronicity. Legacy BERT-based encoders with 512-token truncation limits frequently fail to capture cross-sentence dependencies required to determine whether an extracted finding, such as an osteochondral fracture or radial tear, belongs to the medial or lateral tibial plateau.In contrast, modern instruction-tuned frontier Large Language Models (LLMs), such as Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct, and commercial foundation models, demonstrate advanced contextual reasoning and medical terminology comprehension. Clinical interpretation involves probabilistic hedges, anatomical localization, and gradations of structural damage. Transforming reports into structured supervision requires capturing three complementary dimensions:Epistemic Uncertainty and Continuous Target CalibrationRadiologists convey diagnostic confidence through standard phraseology ranging from explicit negations ("intact," "unremarkable"), to low-confidence hedges ("equivocal signal abnormality," "cannot exclude minor fraying"), to definitive positive assertions ("complete full-thickness tear"). Forcing an ambiguous finding into an absolute $0$ or $1$ introduces artificial label noise that destabilizes visual gradient updates.Instead, the LLM should be prompted to output a continuous posterior probability $p_i \in [0.0, 1.0]$ per finding. Training the visual classifier against continuous probabilities using Soft Binary Cross-Entropy or Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence prevents the loss function from excessively penalizing the model on morphologically borderline cases. For a predicted logit $\hat{z}_i$ and sigmoid probability $\hat{y}_i = \sigma(\hat{z}_i)$, the soft objective is formulated as:$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SoftBCE}} = -\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{12} \Big[ p_i \log(\hat{y}_i) + (1 - p_i) \log(1 - \hat{y}_i) \Big]$$Multi-Class Severity and Structural GradingSeveral knee pathologies represent continuous structural degeneration rather than isolated binary events. Osteoarthritis progresses from joint space narrowing to total cartilage loss; collateral ligament tears span Grade I interstitial sprains, Grade II partial tears, and Grade III complete ruptures; and joint effusion ranges from physiological fluid to massive capsular distension.Structuring the LLM prompt to extract both binary occurrence and ordinal severity establishes intermediate representation learning. Supervising auxiliary heads to classify ordinal severity forces the convolutional or transformer backbone to differentiate between subtle signal hyperintensities and complete ligamentous disruptions, which improves calibration at the binary decision boundary.Anatomical Sub-Compartments and Spatial PriorsKnee pathology is spatially compartmentalized. Meniscal tears occur in the anterior horn, body, or posterior horn of either the medial or lateral meniscus. Osteoarthritis primarily involves the medial femorotibial, lateral femorotibial, or patellofemoral compartments. Bone contusions localize to specific landmarks, such as the lateral tibial plateau or the medial femoral condyle, often accompanying acute ACL ruptures.Prompting the LLM to extract localized sub-compartment coordinates enables multi-task auxiliary heads to constrain spatial feature activations. Aligning spatial visual tokens with extracted anatomical descriptors prevents the vision backbone from correlating extraneous background features or scanner artifacts with specific intra-articular injuries.Noise Robustness and Joint Image-Label Uncertainty ModelingWeak labels extracted from radiology reports exhibit systematic noise stemming from clinical workflow characteristics. Radiologists selectively dictate findings that address the immediate clinical question, often omitting minor, incidental, or chronic conditions, such as asymptomatic Baker's cysts, mild joint effusion, or stable bone contusions. Consequently, an LLM reading an incomplete report will produce an apparent negative target, creating a false-negative label for an image that clearly displays the pathology.Treating weakly extracted targets as ground truth causes deep networks to overfit to label noise, degrading out-of-sample macro AUROC. Mitigating this requires modeling label noise jointly with the visual representations rather than treating the text extraction stage as an unalterable ground truth.Joint Noise Modeling via Confident Learning:

Confident Learning directly estimates the joint distribution between noisy observed report labels $\tilde{y}$ and latent true labels $y^*$ by comparing the weak labels against out-of-fold predicted probabilities $\hat{P}(\tilde{y} = j \mid x)$ generated by an image classifier. The framework establishes finding-specific confidence thresholds:$$t_k = \frac{1}{\vert{}X_{\tilde{y}_k = 1}\vert{}} \sum_{x \in X_{\tilde{y}_k = 1}} \hat{P}(\tilde{y}_k = 1 \mid x)$$When the visual model predicts a high probability of pathology presence that exceeds $t_1$, but the report label is negative ($\tilde{y}_k = 0$), the instance is flagged as a probable false negative (unreported finding). Conversely, if the visual model predicts an absence probability exceeding $t_0$ while the report indicates presence ($\tilde{y}_k = 1$), it is flagged as a probable false positive. Pruning these identified contradictions or reweighting sample loss contributions prevents the image classifier from memorizing clinical omissions.To address class imbalance, Asymmetric Loss (ASL) decouples the focusing hyperparameters $\gamma_+$ and $\gamma_-$ while applying probability margin discounting to negative instances. In multi-label knee MRI datasets, negative findings heavily outnumber positive pathologies. Standard binary cross-entropy allows accumulating easy negative gradients to overpower the sparse gradient updates of positive lesions.ASL suppresses these negative gradients by shifting predicted probabilities:$$\hat{p}_{m,k} = \max(\hat{p}_k - m, 0)$$The loss is formulated as:$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{ASL}} = -\sum_{k=1}^{12} \Big[ y_k (1 - \hat{p}_k)^{\gamma_+} \log(\hat{p}_k) + (1 - y_k) (\hat{p}_{m,k})^{\gamma_-} \log(1 - \hat{p}_{m,k}) \Big]$$Setting $\gamma_+ = 1$, $\gamma_- = 4$, and margin $m > 0$ zeroes out negative gradients for instances where the predicted probability falls below $m$, preventing common negative structures from dominating optimization. In complex radiological benchmarks, ASL improves AUROC and mean average precision by $+1.5$ to $+3.2$ points over standard cross-entropy and symmetric focal loss.Vision-Language Pre-training and Direct Report SupervisionRather than compressing complex radiology narratives into isolated scalar vectors, vision-language pre-training (VLP) optimizes the visual feature extractor directly against narrative text using contrastive objectives. Architectures such as ConVIRT and BioViL maximize the cosine similarity of paired image-text representations while minimizing similarity against mismatched pairs across the batch using an InfoNCE objective:$$\mathcal{L}_{(I, T)} = -\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^N \left( \log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(v_i, t_i)/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^N \exp(\text{sim}(v_i, t_j)/\tau)} + \log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(t_i, v_i)/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^N \exp(\text{sim}(t_i, v_j)/\tau)} \right)$$On the BenchX evaluation benchmark, pre-training an image backbone using ConVIRT improved external downstream pathology classification AUROC by $+5.3$ percentage points ($71.7\%$ to $77.0\%$) compared to supervised baselines. The model operates under a two-pass prompting framework with five-sample self-consistency decoding, outputting calibrated continuous probabilities $\bar{p}_i \in [0.0, 1.0]$, ordinal severity tiers, and anatomical compartment indicators for each finding.In the representation pre-training stage, a multi-plane visual encoder (such as ConvNeXt or Swin Transformer) is initialized and pre-trained directly against the uncompressed text reports using a multi-sequence InfoNCE contrastive objective. Slices from the sagittal, coronal, and axial series are pooled via intra-series attention into a unified study-level image embedding, which is aligned against report embeddings generated by pre-trained PubMedBERT. This step establishes robust visual representations of musculoskeletal tissue contrast prior to task-specific classification.In the noise estimation and dataset purification stage, a 5-fold cross-validation baseline of the multi-plane network is trained to generate out-of-fold predicted probabilities across the training dataset. Cleanlab’s Confident Learning algorithm estimates the class-conditional joint noise distribution between observed text labels and visual predictions, identifying severe false-negative omissions. Flagged clinical contradictions are pruned from the training split, and sample-level confidence weights are assigned to the remaining studies.In the final optimization stage, the contrastive visual backbone is fine-tuned on the purified dataset. The network is configured with primary multi-label heads for the 12 master findings alongside auxiliary heads for ordinal severity and compartment localization. Training is supervised using Dynamic Asymmetric Loss ($\gamma_+ = 1$, $\gamma_- = 4$) incorporating uncertainty margin adjustments to suppress negative gradient overwhelming. During final evaluation on the test set, all auxiliary heads are detached, generating calibrated probabilities solely from the primary heads to maximize macro AUROC across all 12 knee findings.
