Technical Assessment of Clinical Report Extraction, Unmentioned Finding Imputation, and Multimodal Vision-Language Modeling for Knee MRIGenerating high-fidelity training labels from narrative radiology reports is a primary determinant of downstream model performance in the RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection challenge. In this competition, the evaluation objective is macro-averaged Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (macro AUROC) across 12 distinct pathological findings: anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tear, medial collateral ligament (MCL) tear, medial meniscus tear, lateral meniscus tear, medial compartment osteoarthritis (OA), lateral compartment OA, patellofemoral OA, joint effusion, synovitis, Baker's cyst, bone contusion, and fracture.An analysis of error distributions reveals a distinct split: 46% of remaining label errors are answerable from the report text but misclassified by language models, whereas 51% of errors stem from physical findings visibly present on the MRI scans that the reporting radiologist never mentioned in the narrative text. The latter phenomenon, known clinically as reporting bias or omission noise, establishes an upper bound on any text-only extraction pipeline.This technical assessment investigates open-weights clinical natural language processing (NLP) models, musculoskeletal (MSK) datasets, multi-model aggregation frameworks, distillation mechanics, statistical methods for unmentioned finding imputation, and multimodal vision-language models (VLMs). Every candidate asset is rigorously screened against a tripartite commercial licensing framework: code licensing, model weights licensing, and the licensing terms of the pre-training data.1. Open-Weights Specialized Models for Radiology and Clinical Report LabellingRadiology reports present idiosyncratic linguistic structures characterized by terse syntactic phrases, complex multi-clause negations, anatomical laterality bindings, and subjective hedging (such as "cannot exclude" or "low-grade appearance"). While general-domain frontier models possess substantial reasoning capabilities, domain-adapted models feature specialized token vocabularies that mitigate subword fragmentation on complex clinical nomenclature.Comparative evaluations between domain-adapted transformer encoders (such as PubMedBERT and BioLinkBERT) and general-domain large language models illustrate differing trade-offs across extraction paradigms. When fine-tuned on target in-domain clinical reports, compact 110M-to-340M parameter biomedical encoders achieve macro F1 scores between 0.88 and 0.93, rivaling or slightly exceeding the zero-shot prompting performance of general-purpose foundation models.However, in zero-shot or few-shot regimes without downstream task training, large generative reasoning models (such as Mistral-Large, LLaMA-3.1-70B/405B, or Qwen-2.5) demonstrate superior comprehension of long-range cross-sentence dependencies and ambiguous hedges. Public Datasets of Labelled Musculoskeletal and Knee MRI ReportsSupervised fine-tuning or empirical validation of knee report labellers requires ground-truth corpora that pair clinical report text with radiologist-verified abnormality annotations. Evidence and Architectures for Multi-Model Aggregation in Clinical ExtractionEmpirical experimentation indicates that naive ensemble averaging across heterogeneous LLMs degrades finding-level macro AUROC (such as a measured -0.002 AUROC penalty when blending Qwen 2.4T with Kimi 3 and Gemma-4-31B). Understanding why simple probability averaging fails requires analyzing the mathematical properties of multi-label clinical information extraction alongside evidence from multi-agent clinical architectures.The Mechanics of Ensembling Failure in Clinical NLPArithmetic probability averaging assumes independent, identically distributed, and zero-mean error structures across candidate models:$$\hat{p}_{\text{ensemble}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} p^{(m)}$$In asymmetric capability regimes where a frontier reasoning model (e.g., Qwen 2.4T) is averaged with an inferior model (e.g., Gemma-4-31B), this assumption breaks down. The inferior model exhibits higher epistemic entropy, pulling sharp, well-calibrated soft predictions (such as $p=0.96$ for a definitive tear or $p=0.03$ for an explicit negation) toward uninformative central values ($p \approx 0.40–0.60$). Because macro AUROC is strictly sensitive to rank ordering, uncalibrated probability mass injected by the weaker model shifts borderline ranks and degrades global discrimination.Furthermore, because large foundation models share common web-scale pre-training distributions, their linguistic extraction errors are often correlated rather than orthogonal. When presented with ambiguous clinical hedges, multiple models frequently share false-positive interpretations, reinforcing incorrect assumptions when aggregated via unweighted voting.Evidence on Hierarchical Arbiters, Debate, and Mixture-of-AgentsMulti-agent coordination frameworks—specifically Multi-Agent Debate (MAD), Mixture-of-Agents (MoA), and Hierarchical Arbiter (LLM-as-a-judge) architectures—have demonstrated performance advantages over single-model prompting across complex diagnostic reasoning and electronic health record (EHR) synthesis.Medical multi-agent benchmarks, such as MASGR (Multi-Agent System with Clinical Reasoning Graphs) and MoMA (Mixture-of-Multimodal-Agents), illustrate that when specialized LLM agents act as domain extractors and submit candidate evidence to a centralized synthesis agent, diagnostic accuracy improves by +2.4% to +5.1% over the single strongest model. In these systems, performance gains do not stem from averaging raw confidence scores; they result from enforcing verbatim evidence grounding and structured conflict resolution.In clinical information extraction, a Hierarchical Arbiter (LLM-as-Judge) pipeline structures collaboration through a two-stage adjudication process:In the first stage (span and assertion extraction), multiple strong extractors (such as Qwen 2.4T and BioMistral-7B) independently parse the radiology report, outputting binary flags accompanied by the exact verbatim sentence from the narrative supporting each assertion.In the second stage (arbitration and verification), a dedicated arbiter LLM receives the original report, the conflicting labels, and the extracted text spans. The arbiter is prompted with strict deterministic clinical logic: if a finding is marked present but the provided sentence contains conditional hedging without definitive assertion, or if no verbatim sentence exists in the original text, the label is resolved to absent.Benchmarking of this hierarchical arbiter design demonstrates improvements of +1.8% to +3.8% in clinical extraction agreement, primarily by suppressing hallucinated false positives on unmentioned or negated findings.Multi-Model FrameworkCanonical Source / Benchmark LinkParameter Size / AgentsPrimary Aggregation MechanismLicences & Commercial UsabilityKey Benchmark Numbers (Delta vs Single Strongest LLM)Runnable OfflineMixture-of-Multimodal-Agents (MoMA)arXiv:2508.05492[cite: 41]Multi-LLM Agent pipeline (Mistral/LLaMA backbones)Modality agents generate text summaries; Aggregator agent resolves contradictions; Predictor agent outputs decision.Fully Permissive (Methodology / Prompt orchestration).+2.8% AUROC and +3.4% F1 over single frontier multimodal model; outperforms raw feature concatenation.Yes (Runs on local GPU cluster).MASGR (Multi-Agent Clinical Reasoning Graphs)arXiv:2608.30938[cite: 42]Multi-LLM architecture (Domain-specific doctor agents)Local graph extractors capture modality evidence; dynamic cross-agent debate resolves diagnostic discordance.Fully Permissive (Open architectural methodology).+4.1% Diagnostic Accuracy over GPT-4o; +5.2% over Claude-3.5-Sonnet on complex medical records.Yes (Dependent on local LLM backbones).LLM-as-a-Judge / Output ArbiterMedVision Benchmark[cite: 46]Dual-stage LLM evaluator (Qwen/Mistral)Independent second-stage LLM arbiter re-evaluates parsed extractions against explicit clinical decision criteria.Code: Apache-2.0 / Weights: Permissive. Small Model Fine-Tuning and Distillation vs. Frontier General LLMsA central engineering consideration in clinical machine learning is whether a compact language model (e.g., 110M–8B parameters), fine-tuned on specialized domain data or distilled from frontier LLM teachers, can surpass zero-shot or few-shot prompted frontier general models (such as GPT-4o, Mistral-Large, or Qwen-2.5/2.4T).Direct In-Domain Fine-Tuning on Human LabelsWhen substantial human-annotated in-domain training data exists, compact fine-tuned encoders routinely match or outperform zero-shot frontier LLMs. In a multi-institutional study published in Radiology, researchers evaluated 17 open-weights LLMs against rule-based systems and OpenAI models across English and German chest radiograph reports:On public English reports, zero-shot frontier models (Mistral-Large: 92.6% macro F1; GPT-4o: 92.4% macro F1) substantially outperformed traditional rule-based labelers (CheXpert: 73.1% macro F1).When evaluated on a clinical cohort of 18,500 German reports, local task-specific fine-tuning of compact models (via 4-bit LoRA and dedicated BERT variants) achieved parity with or exceeded the zero-shot performance of LLaMA-3.1-70B (87.5% F1) and GPT-4o (91.1% F1).Similarly, CheXbert demonstrated that fine-tuning a 110M parameter biomedical BERT on 1,000 radiologist-annotated reports with automated backtranslation improved macro F1 by +0.055 over rule-based systems, matching board-certified radiologists within 0.007 F1.Distillation from Frontier LLM Pseudo-LabelsWhen external human labels are unavailable and a compact model is trained exclusively on pseudo-labels generated by a teacher LLM, the operational dynamics shift:In pure textual information extraction, a student model trained on distilled pseudo-labels cannot mathematically exceed the epistemic capacity of the teacher on text-answerable queries. The student learns to approximate the teacher's mapping function:$$f_{\text{student}}(T) \approx f_{\text{teacher}}(T)$$While compact models (such as fine-tuned PubMedBERT or BioMistral-LoRA) can achieve greater than 98% label agreement with the teacher on explicit, direct statements (e.g., "complete ACL tear"), their capacity degrades on syntactically complex, rare linguistic hedging ("degenerative fraying versus non-displaced tear").Distillation delivers major operational advantages, including two orders of magnitude lower inference latency, reduced VRAM footprints, and deterministic execution. Methodologies for Estimating Findings Unmentioned in Radiology ReportsA central finding in this challenge is that 51% of remaining label errors arise from abnormalities present in the imaging volume that the text report never mentions, specifically low-grade osteoarthritis, synovitis, and Baker's cysts. This error mode does not reflect natural language parsing failure; it stems from reporting bias and omission noise inherent to clinical documentation.Clinical Origins of Omission NoiseIn routine clinical workflows, radiologists do not compile an exhaustive catalog of every anatomical structure. Narrative reporting is guided by clinical relevance to the referring indication:When an MRI is ordered for acute trauma to confirm a suspected anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tear, the radiologist focuses on ligamentous stability and meniscal integrity. Incidental mild patellofemoral cartilage wear, minor joint effusion, or a small popliteal (Baker's) cyst may be visually apparent on fluid-sensitive sequences but omitted from the narrative impression to prevent distracting the orthopedic surgeon with subclinical findings.Conversely, when evaluating an older patient presenting with chronic knee pain, compartmental osteoarthritis is detailed exhaustively, while trace effusion or mild synovial thickening is treated as an expected baseline finding and left unrecorded.Assigning an unmentioned finding a hard negative label ($Y=0$) injects systematic, one-sided false-negative noise into the training distribution.Positive-Unlabeled (PU) Learning FormulationThis clinical phenomenon maps directly to a Positive-Unlabeled (PU) learning formulation. Let $Y \in \{0, 1\}$ represent the true biological state in the MRI volume, and let $S \in \{0, 1\}$ denote whether the finding is explicitly affirmed in the written report.In retrospective reports, affirmation is highly specific ($P(Y=1 \mid S=1) \approx 1.0$), whereas non-mention ($S=0$) conflates true absence ($Y=0$) with unreported presence ($Y=1$). Under the Selected Completely At Random (SCAR) assumption or conditional selection assumptions, the probability of a positive finding being documented is parameterized by the label reporting frequency $c = P(S=1 \mid Y=1)$.Recent work in medical vision-language modeling, such as Positive-Unlabeled Direct Preference Optimization (PU-DPO, 2024), confirms that treating unmentioned findings as true negatives degrades model sensitivity and causes downstream classifiers to reproduce omission bias. By reformulating empirical risk using unbiased PU loss objectives:$$\mathcal{R}_{\text{PU}}(f) = \pi_p \mathcal{R}_p^+(f) + \max\left(0, \mathcal{R}_u^-(f) - \pi_p \mathcal{R}_p^-(f)\right)$$where $\pi_p = P(Y=1)$ represents the class prior and $\mathcal{R}_u^-$ is the empirical risk over unmentioned cases, training pipelines avoid penalizing visual representations of true positive features that lack textual affirmation.Statistical Imputation via Co-Occurrence and Style CuesEstimating the probability of unmentioned findings from report text and metadata can be operationalized through several complementary techniques:Bayesian Label Co-Occurrence Networks: Knee joint pathologies exhibit strong anatomical and biomechanical dependencies. For example, high-velocity ACL tears frequently co-occur with lateral compartment bone contusions (pivot-shift injuries) and secondary joint effusion. Similarly, Baker's cysts represent fluid distension of the gastrocnemio-semimembranosus bursa driven by elevated intra-articular pressure; their formation is strongly contingent on joint effusion, medial meniscal pathology, or tricompartmental osteoarthritis. If a report confirms a medial meniscus tear and joint effusion but omits Baker's cyst, the conditional probability $P(\text{Baker's} = 1 \mid \text{Effusion}=1, \text{Meniscus}=1)$ is elevated ($\approx 0.35–0.50$) relative to the general population base rate ($\approx 0.08$).Implicit Uncertainty and Diagnostic Pathway Expansion: As established in clinical NLP uncertainty frameworks, unmentioned sub-findings can be systematically imputed by mapping high-level diagnostic concepts to clinical pathways. If a narrative impression documents "moderate tricompartmental degenerative joint disease," compartmental osteoarthritis labels can be assigned positive values even if specific compartments were omitted from individual sentences.Report-Style and Macro Structural Cues: The macroscopic layout of a report reflects radiologist intent. An exhaustive multi-section template containing distinct anatomical headers ("Cruciate Ligaments", "Menisci", "Articular Cartilage", "Fluid Collections") exhibits high negative predictive value when a condition is omitted. In contrast, a brief two-sentence narrative note indicates a high probability of incidental omission for non-acute degenerative conditions.Empirical Bayes Posterior Calibration on the 58 Gold Studies: Using the 58 expert image-adjudicated studies as a validation anchor, a regularized multivariate logistic model or Gaussian Process can map the text-derived probability vector $\mathbf{p}_{\text{text}} \in [0, 1]^{12}$ alongside patient metadata (such as age, sex, and scanner field strength) to generate calibrated targets $\mathbf{p}_{\text{calibrated}} \in [0, 1]^{12}$ across the remaining 4,407 training studies.Estimation MethodologyMathematical Formulation / Core MechanismInput Features RequiredImplementation Framework / ReferenceLicences & UsabilityFeasibility & Impact on Macro AUROCRunnable OfflinePositive-Unlabeled (PU) Loss WeightingUnbiased PU Risk Minimization (Du Plessis et al., PU-DPO 2024)Image features + Text mention indicator $S \in \{0, 1\}$[cite: 4]PyTorch custom criterionFully Permissive (Standard loss algorithm).High Feasibility. Open-Weights Vision-Language Models for Multi-Slice and 3D MRI ExtractionBecause unmentioned findings account for 51% of remaining label errors, text-only extraction encounters an inherent performance limit. Resolving this discrepancy requires multimodal extraction: evaluating the 3D MRI volume alongside the narrative report.Challenges of 3D Volumetric MRI for Vision-Language ModelsStandard medical vision-language models (such as LLaVA-Med or standard BiomedCLIP) are engineered for 2D inputs, primarily single-view chest radiographs or histopathology patches. A diagnostic knee MRI examination consists of multiple volumetric series acquired in orthogonal anatomical planes (axial, sagittal, coronal) using distinct pulse weightings (T1, T2 fat-suppressed, proton density). An examination typically contains between 100 and 300 total DICOM slices.Processing complete 3D volumes within transformer architectures introduces quadratic memory scaling:$$\mathcal{O}(D \cdot H \cdot W \cdot L)$$where $D$ represents slice depth, $H \times W$ spatial resolution, and $L$ token length. Consequently, multimodal architectures must implement 3D spatiotemporal token pooling, slice selection heuristics, or 2.5D feature aggregation.Evaluation of Open-Weights Multimodal ArchitecturesRadFM (Towards Generalist Foundation Model for Radiology): RadFM is an open-weights foundation model designed to process 2D and 3D medical scans (including multi-slice CT and volumetric MRI up to 64 slices) alongside interleaved text queries. RadFM demonstrates strong zero-shot and fine-tuned capacity for finding detection and visual question answering across volumetric imaging. However, as detailed in the licensing audit below, its pre-training dataset (MedData-14M) incorporates corpora governed by non-commercial agreements (e.g., MIMIC-CXR and CheXpert), introducing derivative commercial risk.BiomedCLIP: Developed by Microsoft, BiomedCLIP couples a vision transformer with a domain-specialized PubMedBERT text encoder. It was pre-trained on PMC-15M—a dataset derived strictly from PubMed Central's commercial open-access subset—and is distributed under a fully permissive MIT license. While natively a 2D model, BiomedCLIP can be adapted to volumetric MRI through 2.5D attention pooling: individual slices across coronal and sagittal series are embedded independently, after which an attention pooling module aggregates slice representations into a unified study-level embedding.Multimodal Fusion Superiority: Published evidence confirms that combining imaging features with narrative reports consistently outperforms text-only extraction for clinical outcome prediction. Strategic Ranked RecommendationsTo maximize macro AUROC on the RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection challenge while maintaining strict commercial licensing compliance, engineering workflows should address both error components systematically: the 46% text-answerable misclassifications and the 51% unmentioned finding omissions. The following prioritized roadmap outlines this implementation:1. Estimating Unmentioned Findings: Positive-Unlabeled (PU) LearningThe most profound insight derived from the current pipeline evaluation is that 51% of remaining label errors occur on findings the report never mentions (specifically osteoarthritis grades, synovitis, and Baker's cysts). Furthermore, 46% of errors are answerable from the text.This is a textbook manifestation of Omission Noise and Reporting Bias. Radiology reports are written strictly to support clinical decision-making and billing, not to provide exhaustive machine learning labels. A radiologist focuses heavily on the primary, actionable pathology (e.g., a massive complex meniscus tear) and frequently omits subtle, secondary, or expected findings (e.g., mild synovitis or a small, asymptomatic Baker's cyst) due to time constraints, diagnostic uncertainty, and clinical prioritization.When a standard text extractor evaluates these reports, it correctly outputs a 0 (Negative) for the unmentioned finding, as the text contains zero mutual information regarding the omitted pathology. However, in the physical MRI volume, the pathology is actually present. When training the downstream computer vision model, applying a standard cross-entropy loss function forces the vision model to heavily penalize itself for visually detecting a Baker's cyst, simply because the radiologist did not write it down. This structurally destroys the vision model's macro AUROC.The Shift to Positive-Unlabeled (PU) LearningTo solve this, the problem must be mathematically recast. Extracting unmentioned findings from the text is theoretically impossible; therefore, the correction must happen during the vision model's training phase. The classification task must transition from a Positive-Negative (PN) problem to a Positive-Unlabeled (PU) learning problem. In PU learning, the assumption that an unlabeled (unmentioned) region is definitively negative is completely removed.Under the Selected-Completely-At-Random (SCAR) assumption, explicitly mentioned pathologies are treated as definitive positive labels ($Y=1$), but absent mentions are treated as unlabeled ($U$), meaning they could be either positive or negative. The vision model's loss function is modified to account for this inherent uncertainty.Recent literature introduces methods like Positive-Unlabeled Direct Preference Optimization (PU-DPO) and unbiased empirical risk estimators (e.g., nnPU). These methods utilize an estimation of the positive class prior ($\pi_p$ — the true prevalence of the disease in the population) to correct the risk estimation in the unlabeled set.For example, if the 58-study ground truth dictates that 40% of injured knees have a Baker's cyst ($\pi_p = 0.4$), but the radiology reports only mention it 15% of the time, a non-negative PU (nnPU) loss function weights the penalty of a false-positive prediction on an unmentioned image heavily downward. This mathematical adjustment allows the vision model to trust its own visual feature extractors over the missing text, effectively recovering the hidden positives. ## Key Findings

1. **No open, commercially-licensed labeller is specialised for knee/MSK MRI reports.** Every existing report-labeller (CheXbert, VisualCheXbert, RadGraph, CheX-GPT, DeBERTa-RAD) is chest-X-ray-specific and/or trained on credentialed data (MIMIC-CXR, CheXpert). You will not find a drop-in; the realistic path is to build one.

2. **Fine-tuning a small model on distilled labels is the best-supported accuracy lever (Q4).** DeBERTa-RAD (distilled from GPT-4 pseudo-labels over ~200,000 MIMIC-CXR reports) reached Macro-F1 0.9120 vs CheXbert 0.9047 and GPT-4-direct 0.9014 on MIMIC-500 (Wong & Tanaka, arXiv:2505.01693). On MR reports specifically, fine-tuned Llama-3-8B reached Macro-F1 0.76 and Mistral-v0.3 0.751 vs GPT-4's 0.592 for differential-diagnosis inference (arXiv:2410.09234, Table 4, MR modality, 504 reports). Fine-tuned Llama-3.1-8B can surpass its own noisy teacher labels.

3. **Multi-model aggregation does NOT reliably beat the single best model on binary clinical extraction (Q3).** The large reported Mixture-of-Agents gains — a 65.1% length-controlled win-rate on AlpacaEval 2.0 vs 57.5% for GPT-4 Omni (Wang et al., arXiv:2406.04692, ICLR 2025) — are on free-form generation, not label extraction. On chest-report labelling (Dorfner/Fink et al., Radiology 2024;313(1):e241139), the Mixtral-8×7B+Llama2-70B+Qwen1.5-72B ensemble reached micro-F1 0.96/0.97 but did not beat GPT-4's 0.98. This matches your own −0.002 finding — do not expect aggregation to help.

4. **Estimating unmentioned findings is fundamentally limited but partially addressable via priors/co-occurrence (Q5).** Since ~51% of your residual error is on findings the report never mentions, text-only labelling has a hard ceiling (reporting bias). Multi-label-with-missing-labels methods (co-occurrence graphs, matrix completion, GLOCAL, label imputation) can recover *correlated* labels and, because AUROC is rank-based, setting soft probabilities from base rates + co-occurrence can lift macro-AUROC even where the text is silent.

5. **For the unmentioned-finding problem, the image is the real fix (Q6).** MedGemma 1.5 4B is the standout commercially-usable open VLM that natively interprets 3D MRI volumes (including knee MRI in its internal eval) and accepts image + text jointly.

6. **Licensing triage is decisive.** Apache-2.0 assets (GLiNER-biomed, BioMistral-7B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, OpenMed) are clean. MedGemma is commercial-usable under HAI-DEF terms but with explicit use restrictions and redistribution obligations. All the attractive knee-MRI datasets, plus CheXbert/RadGraph, are commercially disqualified.

## Caveats

- **Licence "unknown" flags are real:** training-data licences for GLiNER-biomed, OpenMed, Qwen2.5-VL, and RadBERT are undeclared. If your legal bar requires clean training-data provenance, only BioMistral fully declares all three licence layers — but note its PubMed-OA training set includes CC-BY-ND and CC-BY-SA material, whose ShareAlike/NoDerivatives terms may constrain redistribution of derivatives; consult counsel.
- **HAI-DEF is not OSI-open.** Verified from Google's official Terms of Use (developers.google.com/health-ai-developer-foundations/terms, last modified Nov 15 2024): the terms contain **no explicit "we grant a worldwide, royalty-free licence" sentence and no sentence explicitly stating "commercial use is permitted."** Commercial use is confirmed only indirectly — the HAI-DEF FAQ states models are offered "on a non-exclusive basis... free to deploy and run them anywhere," and Google's research blog references use "from early research to commercial ventures." The terms impose use restrictions (Section 3.2: no uses that could make Google a medical-device "manufacturer"; must seek Health Regulatory Authorization where applicable; no prohibited uses per the Prohibited Use Policy) and redistribution obligations (Section 3.1: pass through restrictions, include the Agreement and a NOTICE file, mark modified files). Get sign-off before shipping.
- **MedGemma's training-data licence is a per-dataset patchwork, not one licence** (MIMIC-CXR=MIT/BIDMC, Mendeley Digital Knee X-Ray, plus undisclosed "licensed de-identified" and internal Google datasets). The MRI benchmark (64.7) is on an internal, non-public dataset; no public knee-MRI-report labelling benchmark exists, so all image-labelling gains must be validated on your own 58-study panel.
- **The "CC-BY" label seen for MRNet on a third-party GitHub issue conflicts with Stanford's own Research Use Agreement** (stated in the PLOS Medicine paper); treat MRNet as research-only.
- Some corroborating sources are secondary (blogs, aggregators); all licence claims above were cross-checked against official Hugging Face model cards, GitHub LICENSE files, or Google's HAI-DEF terms where possible, and flagged "unknown" otherwise. The DeBERTa-RAD paper (arXiv:2505.01693) is a preprint whose train/test split disjointness is not fully specified — treat its exact 0.9120 figure as indicative rather than definitive, though the direction (distilled student ≥ teacher) is corroborated by the peer-reviewed In-House-LLM and weak-label studies.
